# Well Rounded: the history

How Well Rounded came to be as it is, in the order it happened: the notes kept in the README while it was made (moved here October 17, when the README was cut back to how it is now). Much of it describes what's since been changed or taken out (the watercolour as the default, the challenge, the child's tree, the puzzle, drawn paths, the wall); the README says what's true now.

## To October 16 (the README's section as it stood)


`rings.html` (`src/rings/`) is a third prototype; Atlas Minor and Warm to the Touch are untouched by it. You are one tree, and the screen shows a cross-section of its trunk. **You trace each ring in one calm line round the last, and it's painted in watercolour as you go.** Nothing can go wrong: there's no failure and no score, and the oak grows on as long as you draw. (It was called Annulus; its saves and settings keep the `annulus` names, so trees and plates kept before still load.)

**The daily practice: a ring a day** (`press.html`, `src/rings/press.ts`; the front page of the site and the iPhone app). Inspired by the practice of drawing one circle a day (as in the Zen ensō, which it learns from but doesn't borrow the name, look or meaning of): the tree is kept between visits and each ring remembers its day, so the slice becomes a diary read from the heart outward. Touch inside the slice and the ring under the finger is lit, and its date shown.
* **No line to keep to.** The circle can be drawn anywhere round the slice: where it's drawn is the year's edge, so a loose, wide circle is a wide year and a close one a narrow year (up to six steady widths). No guide, no ink to run out, no failure.
* **Forgiving.** Let go most of the way round (four-fifths or more) and the gap is eased shut and the ring laid; let go sooner and the line rests, to be taken up where it stopped, or begun again by touching anywhere else.
* **Disappearing ink, as a brush.** The line is a brush stroke, wide and wet when unhurried, thin and dry when quick, tapering in; it evaporates over about five seconds, thinning and breaking into dry bristle-streaks. 
* **A painting made to look like wood.** A watercolour study of a slice on good paper, lit softly from the upper left; the slice a little above the middle with room round it. Each ring in oak's straw and honey with its day's season only a tint (a third), a fine umber latewood line (unbroken, never granulated) where the ring is wide enough on the screen to show one, and a painted bark edge, a little uneven. When a ring closes its colour spreads from your line in toward the last ring, a pale first wash deepening; on opening, the slice paints itself in from the heart (`?slow=5` plays both slowly). Each ring's edge is smoothed over 12° of arc and eased 30% toward round, as a tree evens out, and grows a sixth wider on the tree's own side (toward the light, or downhill) and as much narrower on the other, so the heart comes to sit off the middle (not in the challenge), so the slice stays organic and never becomes a lump; the ink stays exactly under the finger. No rays, no relief, no stray marks (a line left resting six seconds fades). A Home Screen icon of a painted slice (`src/rings/icon/`).
* **Wood grown as wood is.** Each ring is laid across itself: pale earlywood, then a narrow band of darker latewood at its edge (the seasons run across a ring, not round it), the whole ring in the colour of its day's season by the calendar (spring green, summer gold, autumn rust, winter grey-violet; northern seasons), so over a year the seasons show as bands. A ring finer than the paper's grain is laid as its blend, so an old slice doesn't shimmer into moiré. Each ring holds a fifth of the game's five-year wood, the crown is grown in a few rings, and the tree never grows old.
* **Never a wait.** As a ring closes (on the iPhone app, a faint touch in the hand) the slice draws back slowly over about three seconds to make room for the next ring, starting from where the new bark was drawn: never a jump. The next ring can be begun at once; a touch while the slice is drawing back stops it where it is. A new tree shows its heart, to draw round; and the ring closes when the finger comes back round to where it began.
* **Quiet.** No sound, no print behind the slice, and no words while a finger is down. One line when it opens ("Today's ring." or "Today's ring is drawn. Another, if you like."), and, under the print, its edition pencilled as a printmaker numbers one: the days drawn on the left, the day's date in the middle, the tree on the right, beside a blind stamp (a small ring slice pressed into the paper, no ink: the maker's mark); written in again, left to right, once a new day's ring has bloomed, and away while a finger is down. Draw as many as you like; a missed day is no matter.
* **Still on a phone.** The page keeps the window's large height (`steady.ts`), so Safari's toolbar coming and going doesn't move the slice, and touches can't scroll, bounce or zoom it. Added to the Home Screen it runs full screen (its storage is then its own, apart from Safari's).
* Kept in the same store as the game's tree under its own key (`diary`). `?fresh` begins another tree; `?demo` shows a year already drawn (not kept).

*(Removed October 16, with its page, code and tests; kept here as history.)* **The challenge** (`challenge.html`, the same drawing and page code, `CHALLENGE` in `press.ts`). Found by playing the practice quickly: ten rings a run, each as close to the last as you dare, and quickly. Each ring scores up to 200: closeness (1 on the edge, a half at 5 px off, a sixth at 25, measured on the screen as the brush went round; over the edge counts as 10 px off, so hugging inside never pays) times pace (2 for a ring in two seconds or less, 1 at four, a half at eight). Each time the brush goes over the last ring's edge it costs 30 from that ring and leaves a small knot in the wood (a soft umber oval along the ring with a pale bloom round it; red dots read as sores); a touch counts only once the brush has been outside the edge, so a finger set down near it (its brush a little way in) costs nothing. (Ending the run at the first touch was tried first; docking points is kinder, and ten-ring runs keep scores comparable.) A new seedling each run, nothing eased toward round, nothing kept in the diary; a ring closes only at nine-tenths round or more. The best score is kept (`annulus.challenge`); a touch a moment after the run ends begins again. Every finished run is kept with a picture of its slice (drawn afresh off the page, centred and whole: `keepRun` in `save.ts`, the last 200): *runs*, at the foot, lays them out best first, each its slice, points and date; touch one to see it large. The practice stays as it was: no score, no failure. On both pages, two fingers pinch the slice closer (up to six times, about where they are), one finger then moves it about, and a double tap goes back (on a computer, the wheel); while it's looked at closely nothing is drawn. The bark always fills at least three-quarters of its room, however close the rings were drawn (they were shrinking it away), and each ring's line is drawn, fainter as the ring is finer on the screen. At the foot of both pages, *practice · challenge* goes between them (the page you're on dimmed); it fades away while a finger is down.

**The relief print** (the default on both pages since October; `?ink=water` for the watercolour; `drawPrint` in `press.ts`), after tree-ring prints, where a slice of the wood itself is inked and pressed. Each year's latewood takes the ink (a band at the ring's outer edge, about half the ring), its springwood stays the paper;  edges are ragged by a fraction of a pixel (on the screen, so the fisheye never magnifies it), and the ink a little starved here and there; rings too fine to print apart show as fine dark lines on the paper; the heart a small dark dot (a tree starts from a 2 mm heart now: a 6 mm one was a third of a young slice); from real oak (a downy oak slice, the Popular Science Monthly plate of pin oak end grain, 1894, and oak at 40× under the microscope): each year begins with a row of large pores, a stipple of dots in its springwood (two rows where it's wide); oak's rays are of two kinds, a few broad ones as long hairlines across many rings and countless fine ones as short pale flecks across a wide band (and as the mosaic of bricks among fine rings); the bark a thick rough band outside the wood (about a twentieth of the radius, 3 to 9 px), fissured, its inner edge a dark line (in the challenge the edge to keep off is the bark's outside); past sixteen years the last eight print a little paler, as sapwood;  All drawn as shapes, so looking closer finds more, never blur (the watercolour is a 512-cell simulation, soft when enlarged). The print is kept as a picture between changes and moved as a picture while the slice moves, so it's cheap to show. Pressed by hand: the ink a little paler toward one side of the slice and in a few broad soft patches (only the ink lightened). The ink never quite covers: paper shows through in specks (about one in every 26 px² of ink), as in a hand-pulled print. **At rest, true to scale:** drawing needs the fisheye (the newest ring magnified under the finger, the old ones squeezed toward the heart), but left a moment the slice settles into true scale over about a second, its years evenly spaced and the bark where it was, and a touch brings the magnifier back in a quarter of a second (cross-faded between two kept pictures; in the challenge only once the run is over). The heart prints as the seedling's first ring (an inked band, paper within, a dot at the centre), never a black disc. In the challenge a touch on the edge splits the wood (since October 12; `drawSplits`): a split opens at the bark where it touched, creeps in toward the heart and widens over some nine seconds, wandering with the grain, and every ring grown after splits too, so a run's touches stay in its slice as the cracks through it (the watercolour keeps a knot); the brush is printing ink, dense while wet, breaking up dry as it fades. Kept runs are pictured true to scale.

*(Removed October 16, with its page, code and tests; kept here as history.)* **A child's tree** (`child.html`, the prototype; `CHILD` in `press.ts`; since October 12). A parent's daily moment of noticing their child, not a tracker, in three scales, each true to trees. **The day:** one gentle question (a different one each day: "What did Ada notice today?"), answered by the loop drawn (loose for an easy day, close for a hard one); if allowed, the sound of the moment shapes it as it's drawn (`ear.ts`: loud swells the ring where it was drawn, hushed pinches it; heard only while drawing, never recorded or kept: only the shape it leaves); then, if they come, a few words, kept with the day and read by touching its ring. **The year:** each year of their life is its own tree, from a fresh seed on their birthday, a ring for each day drawn (each a year of the oak at its own scale; the days after a birthday lighter, those before the next darker); on the next birthday it's finished and kept, a new one begins, and their height is asked. *print* saves the year for printing on real paper, ink only (since October 12): the print, 8 × 10 in at 300 dpi, white (the paper, unprinted) with only the year's tree in ink, true to scale, nothing printed to look like pencil or a stamp (the name and year are for the parent to pencil under it by hand; a stamp, if any, pressed); and its words, a 5 × 7 in card for the back, set plainly as type: the name, the year's dates and days, and each few words kept with their day. And its block (since October 13): the same ink as a laser engraver wants it, to cut into a real wood block and print from by hand: mirrored (a block prints reversed), black where the wood is burned away, white where it stands to take the ink, hard-edged, 600 dpi on a 9 in square with a red hairline to cut the block's edge to. (The idea: the print the app shows is made for real, the year carved in cherry and pulled by hand, by a studio or by the parent on the birthday from a kit.) **Their life:** *every year* shows a tree with a ring for each year of their life (the year in progress as far as it's come): its shape that year's tree's outline, its width how much they grew (3 mm of wood to the cm, measured if their height was given at both birthdays, else as children typically do), so a missed day never narrows a year; touch a year for how much they grew, touch again to open its tree. Kept simple (since October 12): the daily moment is the question, the loop and, if they come, a few words; the rest is quiet. Setup asks their name and birthday, their height if known, and offers (off unless chosen) to let the sound around shape the days; the links under the slice are only *every year · print · keep a copy* (*this year* in place of *every year* when looking elsewhere), and one touch on a year in *every year* opens it; in a year's own tree each month's first day opens a pale line, so a finished year reads as its twelve months, the days fine within them, the gentle lightening across the year kept for the life tree's birthdays; the page asks the browser to keep its storage, and once a month or so of days since the last copy suggests keeping one (and, outside the Home Screen, adding it there); *keep a copy* saves everything as a small file, and setup restores one. `?name=Ada&birth=2024-06-09&demo` plants for a child with every year drawn and a few words kept (not kept). Kept apart from the practice's tree (as `child`).

**A breath** (the practice, printed; `breathe`, `drawBreath`; since October 13). Left still a few seconds, the slice itself breathes: it swells by about one part in sixty over four seconds and settles back over six (six breaths a minute, the out-breath long: what the evidence supports for calm: heart-rate variability rises near six breaths a minute, and a 2023 Stanford trial found five minutes a day of exhale-led breathing lifted mood more than mindfulness meditation), a faint shade round the bark swelling with it; a touch, and it's still. Zoomed in, it breathes too. The first time it breathes it says, once: "Leave it still, and it breathes: in as it swells, out as it settles." No sessions, counts or claims.

**Truer wood** (since October 13): the tree has its own years besides the hand (`climate` in `growing.ts`: runs of good and lean years, a little from year to year, now and then a hard, narrow one), so rings are never evenly spaced; oak's springwood prints as ink with its pores as crisp open holes (the year line a row of pores, not a grey haze); the practice's wood changes scale smoothly out from the heart (each year's from its place and the last eight years' widths), so there's no halo where the slice doubles; and looked at closer than the print's own grain, a finer grain at the screen's scale keeps it crisp. The wood wanders, too: every ring shifts a little with its neighbours, here and there (about a fifth of a year, over a few mm, in the wood's own place, fading out at the bark), so no ring is a ruled curve; and the tree's own years swing wider (a third to twice the hand's).

*(Removed October 16, with its page, code and tests; kept here as history.)* **The puzzle** (`puzzle.html`; `PUZZLE` in `press.ts`, the reckoning in `puzzle.ts`, tested; since October 13). Grow a tree to fill an outline, in eight rings. Today's target is the same for everyone (a perfect circle, an oval, a five-lobed oak, in turn; `?shape=circle|oval|lobes` for any), pencilled on the paper, half again as large as steady years would reach, so it has to be grown into: each ring can only reach so far from the last, so a lobe is built by leaning into it ring after ring, as real trees do. True to scale, no fisheye, no lean or evening-up: the line is exactly yours. After the eighth ring the score is the overlap of the bark with the outline (the area both cover over the area either covers, so overshoot costs as much as falling short), and the best today is kept; *share* makes a card (the slice and the outline, the score, the day) to send or save; a tap tries again.

**The same loop every day; choosing a seed** (since October 13): the bark and the year to come are always shown at the same size (`BARK` of the room), so the day's loop is the same size every day, a ritual's, and as the tree grows the view draws slowly back, the years before tightening toward the heart: growth shows as the rings' density, as a real slice's age does. (A seedling's years are wide beside it, so in its first days the seed sits small in the middle, coming out to meet the loop. Since October 16 the view is never closer than the seed's: the young tree grows out on the sheet until its bark reaches that size, and only then does the view draw back; it used to jump closer after the first ring, then shrink. The view is fitted mostly to the bark's mean, a quarter to its widest (its widest never past nine-tenths of the room), so a lumpy olive or bristlecone no longer looks to shrink as it grows.) A bare sheet shows nine seeds, each a real engraved figure (Faxon's plates for Sargent's *Silva*, the olive from Le Maout and Decaisne) pressed in ink as the slices are, each shown at the one size since October 16, so every tree begins alike (they were in true proportion, which left a birch's seed a speck beside a bristlecone's cone) (`src/rings/seeds/`, `scripts/wood/seeds-*.py`; drawn ones stand in until they load), in rows of three (oak, ash, cherry; maple, birch, pine; redwood, bristlecone, olive), each named in pencil: the one touched is the tree (`Choose a seed.`).

**Just the practice** (since October 13): Well Rounded is the daily practice. Its page links nowhere else; the child's tree, the challenge and the puzzle are kept (their pages still build, at `child.html`, `challenge.html`, `puzzle.html`) but no longer linked, as experiments: the games pulled against what the practice is (slow, true to trees, nothing to win). To begin again: touch the maker's mark (the small stamp at the end of the pencilled line), or the grove's empty circle: nothing is asked, the seeds are shown straight away. The tree drawn so far waits as it was; once a seed is touched it stands in the grove and the new one is planted, and drawn back to the grove instead, nothing has changed (since October 15; it used to ask first). Words only where they're needed: before the first ring (plant a seed; draw a loop round it) and the breath, once; no daily messages.

**Where the code is** (since October 12; since October 16 the practice alone): `press.ts` draws and handles touch for the practice (it once served the challenge and the child's tree too); the reckoning that needs no drawing is apart and tested: `child.ts` (days, a child's age and how it's said, how much they grew, where each day lies in its year, the day's question; `test/child.test.ts`), `growing.ts` (the tree's own slow swells, a season's pace), `ear.ts` (the sound of the moment, never kept), `wood.ts` (the real wood and ink). Browser checks that drive each page are in `test/browser/` (see its README).

**Real wood in the print** (`wood.ts`, `printYears` in `press.ts`; since October 12; since October 15 the child's tree only: the practice's oak and ash are drawn in heavy ink as every other wood is, rays, pores and all, as the scan's tone printed charcoal beside the rest, and the nine choosing slices were pressed again). Oak's years are no longer drawn: each ring takes one real year of a red oak, from the transverse section in Romeyn B. Hough's *The American Woods* (Part I, 1888, plate 15; the plates are thin slices of the trees themselves; public domain, scanned for the Internet Archive; `src/rings/wood/CREDITS.md`). The slice's rings were unrolled straight about the tree's centre, each year traced at its row of pores and straightened to a strip 112 px deep, then turned to ink as a slice of it would print: pores and rays open, the latewood heavy (`scripts/wood/`). Each ring is mapped from its inner edge to its outer, the wood kept to its own proportions round it (so a pore stays round), a run of years sharing one scale so rays run on, a new run where the slice has doubled (as new rays begin); the heart is the first year, from the pith out. Fine rings take smaller copies of the year (down to one row, its mean), so they print as tone, never moiré. Pine keeps its drawn years but takes its grain from a real red pine (plate 19), its ring-lines taken out; birch's scan was too soft to use, so birch is drawn. The real wood loads after the page (about 0.5 MB); until then the drawn print stands in.

**Grown from a seed, true to a tree's scale** (the practice, printed; `GROWN` in `press.ts`; since October 12). Nothing is on the sheet at first: *Tap anywhere to plant a seed*, and the tree grows from there (kept with it, as `at`). The loop is drawn at the hand's size, wherever is comfortable round the slice: how loose it is sets the year's width (46 CSS px out from the bark a steady year; from 0.15 to 3.2 of one), its wobble how the year swells and thins round the tree; once closed, the wet loop draws in onto the slice as that year. The slice is shown true to a tree's scale (0.6 CSS px to the mm) until it fills its room, then fitted to it, so a year is a thin ring and the slice grows slowly, denser year on year, and is looked into closely (a pinch, up to the point a year is some 12 px across). No fisheye. The tree grows its own way as well as the hand's (`growth`): a few broad swells (a lean, a lobe, a flat side) each waxing, waning and turning over months, the lean holding its side longest, and only a gentle evening-up (`evenUp`, in proportion to the radius), so the heart comes to sit off the middle and the outline carries its history, never a fixed pattern. **Pressed:** the ink took or it didn't, the paper's tooth (some 0.12 mm) and the press's wandering pressure deciding where the block was lightly inked, so a pale tone prints as a scatter of ink, never grey; the saw's marks as faint arcs, seen looking close. **Checks:** from about the fortieth year the slice splits as it dries, a crack more every seventy or so (up to five), from the bark in toward the heart, wandering and jagged, widest at the bark, running deeper with the years. **The ink takes as it did on a real print:** how much ink each point took comes from two patches of a real woodcut, Munch's *Two Women on the Shore* (1898; Art Institute of Chicago, public domain): his sea's mottle and sparse specks, his field's grain and streaks (`munch-*-ink.png`, `scripts/wood/inktake.py`), so a solid black prints nearly even, as Vallotton's and Munch's do, and a thin inking breaks into grain and streaks, never a speckle of noise. **A year grows as a season does** (since October 12): once the loop is closed it stays where it was drawn and dries into the sheet, while on the slice the year grows out from the old bark over some seven seconds, quick at first and easing to a stop, the bark moving out ahead of the new wood (`drawGrowing`: the print as it was, and beyond the old bark the print as it is now, drawn a little smaller about the heart and coming to its own size); the years before never move. Room for it is made over a minute and a half, too slowly to see, the print pressed again each second as it goes, and nothing about the print changes when a year is added but the year itself (each run of years takes its scale from its first, the sapwood fades over eight years, the press's patterns are fixed to the sheet), so there's never a pop. A seedling is shown up to three times larger, the boost waning over its first weeks.

**A tree is a year; a copy kept; the opening** (since October 14). A year from its first ring, the practice's tree is finished: on opening, it's found standing in the grove, and the sheet offers the next seed ("A year. It stands in your grove. Choose the next."); planting early is still there (the grove's empty circle). Each finished tree is kept as its own record (`grove/<n>`, with a list of them), so a write touches one tree and none is lost with another; the old single list is moved over when first read. At the foot of the grove, pencilled: *keep a copy* (everything, the grove and today's tree, as a file; typed arrays written out and read back whole, `toText`/`fromText` in `save.ts`, the child's copy too) and *take one back* (a real file button, unseen, over the words: a file can only be asked for from a real touch). The browser is asked to keep the site's storage. Opening (since October 16): today's own tree, once it has three days or more (else one of today's wood, some two months grown in a moment), pressed whole by the slice's own press (`drawPrint`, `inkGrain`) so large its bark is past the sheet's corners, shown ring by ring out from the heart (the ink's edge ragged as it spreads, a paler fringe just ahead), held a breath, then drawn back in one movement to where it lives on the sheet; as it comes into place it gives way to today's own print drawn as large (the print chooses its detail by its size, so the two differ), and lands as that print, so it simply ends: no cross-fade, and no second painting-in after it (a stand-in tree fades into the seeds). About 2.5 s. The grain is pressed a band of rows at a time out from the middle, a few ms a frame, the rings waiting for it, and the opening's pores and flecks are pressed sparse, so it starts at once; nothing under it is pressed while it covers the sheet (today's slice is pressed in its still moment); the paper's tooth is laid over it as over everything. A touch skips it; never under a test or for reduced motion (`?intro` to see it; `?introT=1.9` holds it at that moment, to look at).

**Each wood's temperament** (since October 14; `TEMPER`, `SMOOTH`, `calmOf` in `press.ts`). The gesture is the same for every tree; the wood answers it its own way, never said: only the name is pencilled beneath each slice when choosing, the rest found by drawing. The olive keeps every crooked day: the hand's line is hardly smoothed into its ring (3.5° against 12°) and hardly evened out after. The conifers (pine, redwood, bristlecone) print an unhurried day dark: each ring's latewood band widens and darkens with how calmly the loop was drawn (the tone the tree already keeps per ring), so slow days and quick ones alternate as real latewood does with the seasons.

**Choosing a wood** (since October 14; `drawSeedChoice` in `press.ts`, `src/rings/slices/`). A bare sheet shows one wood at a time: a slice of it as some two months of the practice make it (pressed by the app itself, `?demo=60`, through its print export), as large as the tree will be on the sheet, its name in pencil beneath and a row of dots for where it is among the nine. A finger swipes from one to the next, as a specimen book's pages are turned (arrow keys on a computer; a touch beside it turns too). Touched, it's chosen: the slice draws in, ring by ring, back to its heart, and is its seed (the engraved figure), and the first loop is drawn around that. The breath is no longer announced.

**Steady, closer, and a new tree** (since October 14). From the first ring on, the bark is exactly the same size on the sheet every day (`BARK` of the room), so the day's loop is drawn in the same place every day; the room is made over three and a half seconds as a ring closes, never a minute and a half of creeping growth. Looking closer (a pinch), a loop begun outside the bark is still a ring; begun on the slice, a finger moves it about. The footer counts rings. In the grove, the next tree's ground is a faint pencil circle; touched, a new tree is planted (the finished one standing in the grove); said once, the first time there.

**The grove, from above** (since October 13; `grove.ts`, `drawFar` in `press.ts`). Drawn back past the slice (a pinch to a quarter or so, or the wheel; let go part way and it settles one way or the other), the slice settles into the cut face at the heart of its tree's crown, seen from above, and the view rises off the hill: every tree of the practice, the first planted on the hilltop and the rest spiralling out from it as rings do, today's the newest. The land grows to hold them, never the trees shrinking to fit: each keeps its place and size (world units, `land` in `grove.ts`); twenty to a hill, and when one's full the next rises beside it, the hills spiralling out in turn, so a hundred trees are a wooded country of five hills. The whole is in view when drawn back; a pinch or the wheel goes closer (up to eight times), a finger moves about it, the ground drawn afresh once still (the whole laid under while it moves). A double tap on the ground, home. No dates: the spiral says the order, and a tree touched says its year. It's drawn as an estate map draws a park: the hill in hand-inked contours (its lie from the first tree's seed), each tree its crown with its shadow hatched to the lower right, its kind in its outline and marks (oak broad and lobed, maple dense, cherry small, ash and olive in fine leaflets, birch light and open, pine and redwood tight tufts of needles, the bristlecone bare-limbed on its dying side), as big as its days; a tree of under a month a seedling. Coloured by hand over the ink, as estate maps were (`washCrown`, `washHill`; ink for what stays, watercolour for what changes): each crown a wash of its kind in today's season (spring's fresh green, summer's deep; autumn's russet oak, red maple, yellow ash and birch, rose cherry; winter bare ink but for the evergreens), laid a little off the ink, granulating, dried darker at its edge; today's still wet, with a bloom; the hill a pale wash pooling in its folds (`?season=` to see another). The slices stay ink. All drawn, nothing loaded. A tree touched opens to its slice, to read, not draw (double-tap, back out); today's comes home. Planting a new tree keeps the finished one in the grove (`keepInGrove` in `save.ts`). Nothing is drawn while drawn back. `?grove` plants a grove of five to try it by, `?grove=100` a hundred. (It replaced, the same day, a row of trees from J. G. Strutt's etchings: beautiful, but another look from the slices', and only four kinds had a portrait.)

**Nine kinds of tree** (since October 13; `SPEC` in `press.ts`, `?tree=ash` and so on). Three families of wood: ring-porous **oak** and **ash**, each ring one real year of its wood (white ash from Hough, plate 10, traced as the oak was); diffuse-porous **cherry**, **maple**, **birch** and **olive**, and the conifers **pine**, **redwood** and **bristlecone**, their years drawn and their ink taking the grain of the real wood (Hough's black cherry, broad-leaved maple, paper birch, red pine, and giant sequoia for the redwood; the olive borrows the cherry's grain and the bristlecone the pine's, no section of either being to hand). Each has its own bark (fissured, in plates, papery, the redwood's thick and stringy, the olive's thin) and its own way of growing: the olive gnarled and lopsided (its own swells strengthened, little evening out), the bristlecone's years less than half as wide and, from its thirtieth year, a side dying back to a strip of living bark that widens slowly as it ages (`alive`), the redwood broad, even and calm, without resin canals. A year of daily rings at the grown tree's scale no longer prints solid in oak and ash: rings under a few pixels print at half their tone, a line at as many edges as the screen can part. The footer's date moves aside for a long name.

**Three kinds of tree, first** (`kind` in `press.ts`; a tree keeps its kind, `?tree=pine` or `?tree=birch` plants one, oak by default; the challenge takes `?tree=` too). Each prints its wood as it grows:
* **Oak**, ring-porous: each year opens with a row of large pores; broad rays and fine; a thick fissured bark.
* **Pine**, a conifer: no pores; each year's latewood a crisp dark band of about a third of the ring, with resin canals in it as dark dots ringed with paper; no rays to be seen; a thick bark in plates (fewer, deeper splits, flatter between).
* **Birch**, diffuse-porous: fine pores scattered evenly through the year, each year marked only by a thin line, the ink a little lighter; a thin, pale, papery bark flecked with short dark lenticels.
Where rings are too fine to print apart (under 3 px for pine and birch), they print as a soft tone with ring lines over it, as many as the screen can part: their plain bands, squeezed, shimmered into moiré, and strong lines, each day's wobble its own, crossed into a fingerprint. (Oak's rays break that pattern up, so its fine rings print as bricks.) Drawn from the anatomy (pore arrangement, latewood transition, rays, bark); photographs of pine and birch to check against are still to come (Wikimedia's API was refusing requests).

The first game, below, is kept at `classic.html` on the site.

**The line** (`tree.ts`, `hand.ts`). The finger is the pen (the mouse, dragged, on a computer). The line you draw is the ring's outer edge, low-passed over some fifteen degrees of arc so a shaky hand makes gentle undulations, never spikes. Your pace never changes the width, only how deep the colour is (slower, deeper). A faint pencil circle round the pith shows where an even ring would land, and can breathe slowly (about six breaths a minute) as a suggestion of pace. Lift your finger to rest; touch the drop to go on. Weather never pushes the line, nothing dies, nothing runs out, and old age doesn't come: the tree ends only when you choose *keep as a plate*.

**The watercolour** (`wash.ts`, `paint.ts`): a small simulation of water and pigment on paper, and the wood's look is what the paint does.
* **Disappearing ink.** The line goes down as dark, wet ink that evaporates behind the pen over a few seconds, like water on a calligraphy board, leaving the colour it bled into the paper.
* **The paper stays wet while you paint a ring**, however long you take, so its start and end meet wet-in-wet. When the ring closes it dries over a few seconds, and as it dries its pigment gathers at its edges (the coffee-ring effect): those dark dried rims are the rings.
* **Wet-in-wet.** Begin the next ring while the last is still damp and their colours run into one another; wait, and the edge between them stays crisp.
* **The season while wet, the wood when dry.** Round each ring the season turns as you draw: spring's sap green, summer gold, autumn rust and madder, a winter violet, and back toward green where it closes. As the ring dries, its pigment settles into the colour of oak, keeping only a whisper of its season, so the slice is wood while the ring under the pen is alive with colour. The heartwood deepens to a warm brown as the tree ages. Each tree to come will have its own wood (yew orange and purple, cherry red, birch silver), so the library of plates, not any one tree, is the spectrum. The weather tints the year and changes how the paint behaves:
  * rain is cooler and wetter, slower to dry, and blooms, and dries a greyer wood;
  * drought is a thin ochre that dries fast, granulates, and dries a golden wood;
  * frost is a pale, cold blue, drying grey;
  * wind drifts the wet colour downwind;
  * a fire leaves a vermilion and sepia bloom on the uphill side.
* Pigment granulates into the paper's tooth, the paper's texture lies faintly over the sheet, and re-wetting dried paint lifts some of it again. Colours mix as paints do (by absorbance), never as digital gradients.

**The camera** keeps the ring you're drawing large and comfortable: the slice fills its room, and a gentle fisheye widens the newest rings on an old trunk, so the colour lands where you trace and the old rings grow small toward the pith. The plate is drawn true to scale. Engraving (fine ink lines, no paint) is still in *settings*.

**Calm.** Between rings, a beat of about two seconds while the season turns in the habit drawing. The pen is heard on the paper, the rain and wind in the habit drawing, and a soft tick sounds as a ring closes (*settings* has a mute). On the iPhone, a light haptic texture in the hand while the pen moves. Weather comes only every several rings, signalled a ring or two ahead in the habit drawing.

**From the artist's own prints** (since October 17). Looking at real tree-ring prints, four things a real pressed slice has that ours didn't: the tree leans, so its heart sits off the middle and its years run wide on one side and close on the other (`leanOf`: none for the first dozen years, gathering over the next sixty, a third to two-thirds wider on its own side); the bark parts from the wood as the slice dries, a broken hairline of paper just inside it; a faint grey stipple just beyond the edge, ink caught from the block's rim; and broad patches where the ink starved, broken by the paper's tooth into speckle. The choosing slices pressed again with them.

**Real ink** (since October 17). The ink's speckle is lifted from photographs of real tree-ring prints: the flecks of paper where the ink broke on the wood, in place of Munch's sea (`ink-speckle.png`; `?ink=munch` for the old).

**Rays and solid ink, as a real print has** (since October 17). Every wood now prints its rays (`rayAt`): hairline radial lines open to the paper, a few from the heart and new ones beginning between them as the wood widens, so they keep about the same distance apart all the way out, each wandering a little and breaking now and then; plainest in oak (its latewood a run of small bricks), faint in the conifers (`RAYS`). And the ink is laid more solidly: the press's starving of it held back, so the black is black, broken only by small flecks, its edges crisp.

**Cracks from the heart, and stepped** (since October 17). An old slice now splits from the heart too: from about its ninetieth year, two to four cracks open out from the pith, parting it into wedges. And a crack, from the bark or the heart, now and then runs a short way along a year's edge before going on, as wood parts along its rings as readily as across them: a step. Each crack is stroked a piece at a time, as wide as it is there, so it turns without a seam.

**Sharp up close** (since October 17). Looked into, the print was soft: pressed at two device pixels to a CSS px and shown on a phone's three, so stretched by half; the drawn woods' fibre, from an old scan, magnified into diagonal smears; the rays and the edge's specks sized with the wood, so thick. Now the print is pressed at the screen's own resolution (`QUALITY`, the strips smaller so a frame's share stays small); the scan's fibre is used lightly, under a finer grain of the wood's own that only shows as it's looked into, so closer finds more, never a blur; the rays are finer, their edges ragged; the specks grow less, and aren't round.

**Steady when looked into; rays as they run** (since October 17). Pinching counted as drawing, so the slice's magnifier came back under the fingers and, a moment after they lifted, eased away again: the heart's years swelling outward, the slice seeming to zoom of itself. Now only drawing brings the magnifier back; while looking closer it holds as it is. And the rays were ruled straight from heart to bark; now each bends a little and jogs as the years about it swell and pinch, and breaks off: oak's in long runs, the other woods' in short flecks a few years tall, staggered (`RAY_RUN`).

**Douglas fir and juniper; birch let go; fine years as hairlines** (since October 17). Two woods, each a look the others hadn't: Douglas fir, the boldest year of all, a pale earlywood closed all at once by a dark latewood (`sharp`), a thick furrowed bark; and juniper, close fine years and a fluted trunk, its lobes and bays deepening with age (`fluteOf`), a thin shredding bark. Birch is no longer offered (its print too plain to be worth the choosing); a birch already grown is kept. And a slice's fine years, too narrow to print apart, printed near black; now they're hairlines, the line on each year's edge never more than a quarter of it, so an old slice reads as fine grey lines.

**Fir's and juniper's seeds from the plates** (since October 17). Their seeds were drawn by hand in the app; now they're cut from Faxon's plates for Sargent's *Silva*, as the others are: the Douglas fir's hanging cone, its three-pointed bracts showing (Tab. DCVII), and two berries of the eastern red cedar (Tab. DXXIV), pressed the same way (`seeds-cut.py`, `seeds-press.py`, `ONLY=fir,juniper`).

**An even pace, and a brush's line** (since October 17). Motion stuttered: left alone, the frames came half as often, but the slice breathes and a year grows then, so it ran by turns at sixty and thirty; and the sharper print was pressed in a slice of each frame on top of the frame's own work, past the phone's sixteen milliseconds. Now every frame is drawn, and the print is pressed only in what the frame leaves over (a strip at least every quarter second, so it always comes). And the day's line, an even marker's stroke kinked wherever the finger wavered, is a brush's: the hand's path eased round its wavers, fine where it touched down, swelling and thinning as it goes, drawn to a point at its head while it moves, its edges a little ragged, and where the hand hurried the bristles run dry and leave the paper between them.

**Looked into, still** (since October 17). Two things moved a slice looked into after the fingers lifted: the sharper print, pressed for the view, replaced the enlarged one all at once, its rays and grain (too fine to see before) appearing in a frame; and the breath, a sixtieth's swell, enlarged three times over into a pumping of the lines. Now the picture replaced fades out over the new one (`fading`, some half a second), and the slice doesn't breathe while it's looked into.

**Zoom that holds, a slice its own size, a bolder fir** (since October 17). A ring closed, the slice is shown where the loop was drawn and draws back to its own size over a minute and a half; a pinch then froze it there for good (so a slice could stay the size of a wide loop, filling the sheet), and the year went on growing under the fingers, the rings sliding out after the zoom. Now a pinch finishes the drawing back at once and makes the look that much closer instead (`holdForLook`), so nothing moves under the fingers or after them, and zooming out finds the slice its own size; a year still growing is grown. A wide loop's year is shown at most three-tenths larger at first. And Douglas fir's years were near bare paper with a thin line; now each is a broad dark latewood building from a speckled earlywood, as a fir prints.

**Less texture** (since October 17). Looked into, the drawn woods read as corrugated card: the wood's fine grain along each year, and fir's fibre on top of it, too strong. Both laid more lightly (the grain at two-fifths, fir's fibre at under half), so a year looked into is smooth ink with a soft edge where it begins.

**The grove as an estate map** (since October 17). Close up the grove read as a vector drawing, and seen whole as a scatter. Now: each crown's edge is small scallops round its leaf-masses (a conifer's its tufts), its masses' shaded sides drawn within it, its marks leaf-clumps of two or three small scallops (looked into, single leaves; a conifer's, needle sprays), its outline lighter; the shadows hatched by hand, each stroke its own spacing and weight and broken now and then. The ground quieter: fewer contours, each laid as one line so it never beads, fine and pale; a meadow's stipple, thicker in the low ground, that looked into is tufts of grass (`drawMeadow`). The stream a stream: its width wandering, a cool wash paler down its middle, broken pen banks, faint current lines, reeds and a stone, its damp margin narrower. The grove tighter (`SPACING` 1.45), a young tree's crown never under some half its full size, and round it the wood it stands in: trees no one planted, older, inked and faintly washed, thinning with the distance, never on the stream or the next tree's ground, and not to be planted on.

**October 17: a calmer grove.** The crowns stopped staggering. Each tree had its own phase, and the sway grew with zoom. Now one slow wave of wind moves across the grove, at most a fraction of a pixel. Shadows are swept from the trunk along the sun, so they never come loose from the tree in the evening, and they're hatched by hand as one layer instead of one per tree. Zooming in costs about half as much: crown washes are painted at a few fixed sizes and reused, the marks per crown are capped, and the wild trees take a flat wash.

**October 17: zooming without a freeze.** A view zoomed into is no longer drawn all at once. Its pieces (the ground, the water, the shadows, then each crown) are drawn in whatever time each frame has left. Until it's finished, the last view is shown scaled, and then the new one fades in over it. Crown washes no longer paint the empty corners round each crown. At 4× slowdown, the longest frame while zooming went from about 2.5 s to 0.4 s.

**October 18: sharp sooner when looked into.** After a pinch in, the print stayed a stretched, stair-stepped copy of the unzoomed one for many seconds. Re-pressing every device pixel of a zoomed screen took around 10 seconds on a phone. Now:
- A zoomed print is pressed less finely, down to a pixel and a half per CSS pixel at deep zoom, because there the wood's own scan is coarser than the screen.
- It's pressed from the middle of the screen outward, and each finished band is shown as soon as it's done.
- Each strip is as deep as about fifteen milliseconds of pressing allows.
- The wood's rays skip the noise work wherever no ray can reach.
At about 5× zoom, the time to press the whole screen fell to roughly a quarter of what it was.

**October 18: zooming without a swap.** Looking closer used to stretch the print and then swap in a re-pressed one, and the two didn't quite match.
- The few marks still sized by the screen are now sized by the wood: the stipple round the bark, the birch's bark lines and lenticels, the close-up grain, and the saw marks. A view pressed closer is the same picture, only finer.
- Once the slice has rested a moment, the print is pressed again quietly in spare frame time. This fine copy is about 2.5× finer and covers the slice only, pressed exactly as a look that close would be. Pinching in then only magnifies it: nothing is re-pressed and nothing changes, up to where a fresh press would be no sharper. It's dropped whenever the years, the wood's scans, or the sheet change.

**October 18: the press on the graphics chip.** The print's per-pixel work moved to the phone's graphics chip (WebGL2, src/rings/gpu.ts): the years (each wood's anatomy, its scans, the rays, pores and fine lines) and the ink's grain (the speckle, the paper's tooth, the saw). A whole screen is pressed at once, in milliseconds rather than seconds.
- Every view is pressed at the screen's own resolution.
- A pinch is pressed again as it goes, so the print under the fingers never goes soft.
- The workarounds (strips, lower density when zoomed, the fine copy pressed at rest) are kept only for phones without WebGL2, which press by hand as before; `?cpu` forces that, to compare.
- The chip can't reproduce the CPU's sine-based hash, so a tree's pores and rays fall in slightly different places than they did by hand. Each wood's look is the same, checked side by side for all ten.

**October 18: the print live under the fingers.** On the graphics chip, a view is now pressed again for exactly what's shown, each frame as the fingers move, so a pinch never shows a stretched picture. When a press takes longer than a frame has, it's pressed every other frame or less, so the pinch itself never stalls. The ink's grain now reads the canvas on the chip itself, never copying it back to the CPU. Only the outlines (bark, cracks, the stipple) are still drawn by the CPU, in a few milliseconds.

**October 18: the grove's ground and washes on the graphics chip.** The grove's per-pixel work moved to the chip too.
- The ground is drawn in one pass: the hill's wash, its winter snow, the meadow's specks (and its grass tufts when looked into) and the hand-drawn contours, broken now and then as before.
- Each crown's watercolour is painted on the chip, and larger than before (up to 360 px across its radius), so it holds up looked into.
- The CPU now does only the ink: crowns, shadows, the stream, the paths.
- The chip's noise is its own, so the hills' contours lie a little differently than they did. Where there's no WebGL2, or with `?cpu`, the grove is drawn by hand as before.

**October 18: the breeze, looked into.** Zoomed into the grove, one finger used to move the view about, and a breeze needed a moment's hold first. Now one finger is always the breeze, looked into or not: the crowns lean, leaves lift, the water ruffles. Two fingers move the grove about, pinching or not.

**October 18: no growing out; knots pressed at once.**
- The year no longer grows out from the old bark over seven seconds once a loop is closed. The new ring is simply there where it settled, the print as it was fading out over it.
- The print's key now includes its knots and burls. One begun or grown is always pressed in straight away, never only once the slice is looked into.

**October 18: the relief-print look.** The drawn woods' years were redrawn after the artist's own prints, side by side with the photographs.
- Each latewood is a bold band of ink, its inner edge never ruled: it wanders along the year and frays finely at the grain.
- Beside it the earlywood is mostly paper, with ink bled into it in blotches. Inside it the band is starved here and there to flecks and short streaks of paper, and finely all through it to the wood's pores.
- The rays are fainter in the drawn woods, so the years' bands run on through them.
- The latewood share is wider for cherry, maple, birch, olive, juniper and redwood.
- Checks open in from the bark from about the twentieth ring, not the fortieth.
- The CPU press matches the graphics chip's, and the choosing slices were pressed again in the new look.

**October 18: a seamless draw again.** Closing a loop had begun to stutter: since the press moved to the graphics chip, every re-press (the new year, the magnifier easing off, the slice settling) was done whole in one frame. On a phone each was a visible hitch.
- Now only a live pinch presses whole. Otherwise the print is pressed a strip at a time in each frame's spare time, the last print shown meanwhile and then fading out over the new one.
- The grove drawn ahead in the background, for pinching out to, is also drawn a piece a frame, never in one go.

**October 18: the new year there at once.** Closing a loop showed the old slice inside the loop for a moment while the new year was pressed a strip at a time, then the year popped in. On the graphics chip the year just closed is now pressed at once, so the loop dries onto a print that already holds it. Other presses still come a strip a frame. A canvas still fading out, or shown by another slot, is never pressed into, and bands pressed for a view the slice has moved from are never laid over it.

**October 18: no bar while it sharpens.** While a print was pressed again, its finished bands were laid over the softer picture from the middle out. On a phone, where that takes many frames, the edge between them showed as a horizontal bar twitching up and down. A sharper print is now pressed out of sight and fades in once it's whole. On the graphics chip, a re-press now covers the whole picture, never leaving a stretched copy's edge in the margin to drift into view.

**October 18: a print, looked into.** Six changes after looking closely at a phone screenshot beside the artist's prints:
- The ink's texture is now laid round each ring at the ring's own radius. Before, it used each pixel's distance from the heart, which sheared it into slanted streaks that read as brush strokes. The bands' edges tear in short bites, and the paper shows through as small specks, not dashes.
- Every year is its own: some thin and pale, some broad and heavy.
- The pale wood between the bands carries the grain's fine lines, faintly, along the year.
- Bleed beside a band is black specks and blots, never grey. The starved patches are much fainter, and the ink-take texture is gentler.
- The bark is a thinner, more broken band, and the stray ink round it is finer, sparser and dragged outward.
- "Double-tap to zoom out" is said only the first three times ever.
- The CPU press matches, and the choosing slices were pressed again.

**October 18: oak, ash and pine as prints too.** The scanned woods now print as the drawn ones do. Each year is its own, its latewood broader or narrower and heavier or lighter, its edge torn in short bites. The ink is taken or not, never a grey wash, starved here and there to small specks of paper. The scan's own grain, pores and rays are kept inside the bands.

**October 18: tidied.** Dead code is gone: the growing-out animation, and the fine copy that was pressed at rest, which since the graphics chip only ever helped the fallback. The wood's anatomy, its rays and the grain's noise moved out of press.ts into anatomy.ts.

**October 18: the grove's ink lighter to draw.** The ground pass works the hills' height out once a pixel, where it took some fifteen goes before, with its slope from the graphics chip's own neighbours. It costs about half what it did. The shadows' hatching is done on the chip too: their shape laid as a mask, the strokes drawn inside it all at once, each a little off true and broken now and then, instead of thousands of strokes by hand. The crowns, stream and paths are still drawn by hand, a tree a frame, small enough not to show.

**October 18: no rim across a check.** The press mark, the paper's step down round the block, traced a disk the size of the wood. Where a check cut through the bark, that disk's rim showed inside it as a faint line across the wedge. The checks are now kept as they're cut and filled into the block's shape for the mark, within the bark, so a check opens cleanly as a notch in the edge.

**October 18: the wet line breaks at a check.** The loop drying onto the slice is no longer laid over a check where one opens through the bark: the line breaks off at the check's mouth, as ink does over a split in the block.

**October 18: words never over the print.** A line of words is now withheld while the slice lies under it, as when looking closer brings the slice up beneath "Double-tap to zoom out.", and it comes back once the slice is drawn back. Out in the grove, where the map runs to the sheet's edge, the words and *keep a copy* / *take one back* are written on a small clearing of paper of their own.

**October 18: the opening as dark as the print.** The opening pressed its huge slice as if seen whole, so every speck of paper, fray and grain was sized for a small print and came out fine and pale at that size: lighter and grainier than the slice it settles into. It's now pressed as the slice looked into closely (up to about three times, as far as a pinch goes), so its marks are the finished print's, larger. On the graphics chip it's pressed at the screen's own resolution, all at once.

**October 18: no dark flash as a print is replaced.** When a print gave way to a newer one, the old one was laid at full strength over the new and faded. The two, a hair out of register, added up their ink, so the slice flashed darker for half a second after each ring. Every change of print is now a true crossfade, composed aside: the replaced print's fade, and the slice settling from the magnified print to the true-scale one.

**October 18: a still slice, a still grove, and leaves that come down.**
- The slice no longer eases back a hair a frame after a ring, nor swells with the breath. Redrawn a hair larger or smaller each frame, a sharp print shimmered. A small step back is now a crossfade from the print as it was, at its own size, into the new one. The breath is a soft shade in the paper round the bark, widening and ebbing.
- The grove's trees no longer sway on their own; they move only in a breeze drawn across them.
- In autumn the broadleaves' leaves lie round them, at their drip line and thickest on the lee side, more as the season goes on (by the date). Now and then one comes out of a crown, swinging and turning over as it falls, and lands among them. In spring the cherry's petals do the same.

**October 18: the rays keep their places.** The rays, and the year's fine grain and pores, were laid by where a point fell on the screen. Every year added, the slice draws back a little, so every ray in the slice was laid anew and the lines jumped. Real rays never move: each runs on into the new year, and a few new ones begin as the trunk widens. Now the rays are laid in the wood's own millimetres, so the old ones keep their places and new ones begin only in new wood, more of them as the slice grows. The finer grain is laid at the slice's own scale to the nearest doubling, so it keeps still from day to day too. (A ring added, the wood inside it matched its last print 0.75 before, 0.92 now for cherry and 0.95 for oak.) The breath's shade round the bark is fainter.

**October 18: the year grows out.** Closing a loop, the new year's wood now grows out from the old bark over some two and a half seconds, quick at first and slowing as a summer does. The bark is carried out on its edge, and the drawn line draws in to meet it. The slice itself stays still while it grows: the years before never move, and the view draws back to make room only once the year is in.

**October 18: oak and cherry, looked into.**
- Rays had come to be drawn in the wood's millimetres, width and all, so looked into, or on a young tree, they were wide white channels cutting the bands into bricks. They now keep their places in the wood but are drawn the screen's width: fine, varied, tapering where they begin and wandering a little. They broaden only a little as the slice is looked into, and end short of the bark.
- Oak's and ash's scanned wood is now read a little either way along the year and averaged. Its own pores and rays, enlarged, no longer make a comb of teeth on each band's edge or a row of white channels. Their bands are starved here and there to flecks of paper, never a flat black block. Their heart is a pale pith ringed by its first dark edge, never a stretched, smeared copy of the year.
- The drawn woods' bands (cherry's, maple's, the rest) were starved to a fine sand of paper. Their flecks are now fewer and larger, so the bands read solid, as the artist's prints do.
- The choosing slices were pressed again.

**October 18: no ghost when a ring is closed looked into.** Drawing while looked into, the print as it was faded out at its own place while the new one stood elsewhere, for a moment two slices, one over the other. The print as it was is now kept where it stood only when the slice hasn't moved; otherwise it fades over the new one where the new one is.

**October 18: shadows unbroken, leaves dropping close.** The shadows' hatch strokes were all broken at the same heights, so their gaps lined up into blank rows across each shadow; each stroke now breaks at its own places. A falling leaf now drops from the crown's edge just above where it lands, swinging a little, never flying across the tree.

**October 18: every knot its own.** Knots were one target, stamped: a round, dark ring of rings about a dot. Now each is its own. It's as long as its branch met the cut at a slant, from round to a long streak pointing out from the heart, and as large as it grew. Its rings are fine, uneven and now and then broken off, and set off centre (a branch grows more on its upper side) about a small pith. A live knot's rings run on into the trunk's. A dead one sits in a dark ring of the bark it was buried in, shrunk from the wood along one side and split across. (`?demo=40&knots=7` grows some in, to look at.)

**October 18: a grove that settles, and leaves that drift.**
- When a view of the grove was drawn for itself it was laid over the last at full strength as it faded in, and their inks added up. A view counted as current only within half a pixel, so a finger settling set it drawing anew again and again, and the grove flickered darker and lighter. Now the views are crossfaded, and a view holds until the grove has really moved (some 3 px, or 1.5% in scale).
- Crossfades compose each picture whole on its own sheet before adding the two, so one's crowns are never added onto the other's ground.
- A falling leaf no longer shoots out of the crown and slows. It lets go slowly and comes down at a gentle, steady pace, rocking side to side and now and then turning over. It's a little larger while high, and its faint shadow closes in on it as it lands.

**October 18: knots as they print.** Knots had become outlined ovals of rings laid on the print, sometimes past the bark. A knot's wood is denser than the trunk's and takes the ink heavily, so it now prints as a dark, uneven mass, flecked with paper. Its own rings are only a few fine pale traces, broken, off centre, and the trunk's rings sweep round it, as a burl's do round its eyes. A dead one is still shrunk from the wood along one side and split. Knots always lie within the wood, never across the bark.
