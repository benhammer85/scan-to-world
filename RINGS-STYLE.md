# Well Rounded: the style rulebook

Well Rounded is drawn by [STYLE.md](STYLE.md), Atlas Minor's rulebook, with one change: its test.

## The one test

> **Would a botanical engraver of about 1820 have drawn it this way?**

The references are the plates of transverse sections in the botanical and forestry books of about 1780 to 1850, the small habit drawings of the whole plant those plates set beside their details, and the wood specimens of the xylotheques of the same years: earnest drawings of the newest science, not old-looking ones.

## What that means here

- **Ink only, two line weights.** The fine line for the rings, the line being drawn, the bark's fissures and all hatching; the heavy line for what matters most: the bark's outer edge, a fire's scar, the dead surface where the cambium died, a thick limb's shaded edge. (The rings are all fine: a heavy latewood stretch on rings five years wide drew every ring as a dark spiral arc.)
- **Every ring a smooth curve.** A ring is drawn as a smooth closed curve through its points, never a polygon, and its edge is the hand's line low-passed over some fifteen degrees: a shaky hand gives gentle undulations, as real rings are uneven, and never spikes. An engraver drew a section's rings as flowing lines, not a starburst.
- **Wounds lie along the ring, never across it.** Where wood died it is a soft dark band along its ring, hatched along its length and tapering at each end where the callus rolls over it; the rings after it close in round it. Never a radial wedge or a spoke from the pith.
- **Hatching, never gradients.** The slice's shadow is parallel strokes outside it, away from the light (upper left). A fire's char is cross-hatching. The wet line's wood is radial hatching from the bark to the edge, broken where the brush's wood is spent. In the habit drawing, the hill and the crown's shaded side are hatched.
- **Earlywood to latewood, pale to dark,** in steps round each ring the way its stroke went, and darker where the hand went slowly (the pace's only mark: it never changes the width). Where the stroke began and ended, its seam shows, as an ensō's does.
- **Signs and scenes, not words.** The sun is the astronomer's sign, a ring with a dot, rayed in a drought. Decades are the dendrochronologist's pricked dots. Where an even ring would land is a dotted pencil circle round the pith, an engraver's construction line (it may breathe, slowly, as a suggestion of pace). The wood left in the brush is a drop at the pen's tip; resting, the drop waits. The weather is never written: it acts on the line, and it happens in the habit drawing (clouds and ruled rain, heat shimmer and cracked ground, wind streaks, a ruled cold sky and hoar, smoke and tongues of flame). No icons, no words on the slice.
- **The oak as the page, from real prints:** the whole oak stands up the screen behind the slice, drawn from real plates of 1805 to 1825 (Billington's woodcuts, Delamotte's soft-ground etching, Strutt's *Sylva Britannica*), one for each stage of its life, printed faint in their own line on the game's paper; growth figures of the tree at its ages are added as it reaches them, as Loudon's plates of 1838 drew a tree at its ages. Never imitate an engraving where a real one can be had; always credit the engraver. The older small habit drawing was a short bole and heavy limbs in ink, foliage as an engraver touches it (scalloped leaf-masses, a few loops of leaf within, the side away from the light hatched), the hill's line heavy and hatched beneath. It grows with the rings and turns with the seasons.
- **Watercolour is the wood's own look** (the default; engraving is the alternative). The paint is simulated, never drawn as a picture of paint: water and pigment spread, mix by absorbance as paints do, bloom, granulate into the paper's tooth, and as a wash dries its pigment gathers at its edge (the coffee-ring effect): those dried rims are the rings. The traced line is disappearing ink, dark and wet at the pen and evaporating behind it; only the colour stays. The seasons colour each ring round its loop while it is wet (sap green, gold, rust, madder, a winter violet), and as it dries it settles into the colour of the wood, keeping a whisper of its season: the slice is oak, not a rainbow. The heartwood deepens with age. The weather tints the year: rain cool and full, drying grey; drought thin ochre, drying golden; frost pale; a fire's bloom vermilion and sepia, drying to charcoal. Wet-in-wet where a ring was begun while the last was damp, crisp where it had dried. Never a digital gradient. Ink for what stays, watercolour for what changes (the volcano's rule, VOLCANO-STYLE.md):
- **Fading ink, as STYLE.md says:** rings out of view (when you come close) hold 25 s, fade over 45 s to pencil, never below, and are inked again when back in view.
- **One motion at a time.** Only the pen moves for attention while a ring is drawn. The ink coming back to a faded ring has no nib.
- **Out, always:** the same traps as STYLE.md. No bark texture photographs, no wood-grain gradients, no knots drawn for charm. Wobble only where a hand put it, gentled as a pen's line is: the stroke's path, low-passed, never jittered or blotted by the program.

## The daily practice

The front page is held to a second test as well: **would it let you breathe while you draw?** Anything that makes the hand careful, or the eye leave the slice, fails it.
* Nothing to hit: no guide circle, no band to keep to. Where the circle goes is the year's edge.
* Nothing to read while a finger is down, and nothing behind the slice: no print, no weather across the page, no sound.
* A painting made to look like wood, never a wooden object: watercolour on paper, no relief, bevels or highlights (they read as plastic), no shadows of its own. The season runs across a ring (pale earlywood, a fine umber latewood line at its edge), never round it, and is only a tint in oak's straw and honey.
* Nothing that reads as a scratch: no rays on a young slice, no stray marks left on the page.
* What's too fine to draw is drawn as its blend, never as shimmer.

## Inspired by, never pretending to be

Well Rounded learns from many traditions and dresses up as none of them. Its own lineage is the naturalist's printmaking practice: relief prints taken from real wood (tree-ring prints), the wood-anatomy plates of the nineteenth century, the botanical habit drawing, and the daily nature journal (Thoreau's at Walden, Gilbert White's at Selborne).
* Borrow principles and craft, never another culture's symbols or sacred practices as decoration: no seals posing as hanko, no borrowed calendars of another climate, no brush "Asian" lettering, kanji or red suns, no ensō in the name, look or words.
* Where a technique has a name in our own tradition, use it (a blend roll, not bokashi; cotton rag paper, not washi; a printer's blind stamp and a pencilled edition, not a seal).
* Say where the ideas came from, plainly, in a quiet line the player can find.
